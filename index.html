02 Experience

  1. Jun 2026 – Aug 2026

    Boston, MA

    AI Intern

    Locust Walk

    As an AI intern at Locust Walk, a biopharma advisory firm in Boston, I built an automated pipeline that finds executives and board members at target companies and prepares them to load into the firm’s CRM.

    The problem The CRM’s contact data was incomplete and going stale, and keeping it current meant researching one person at a time by hand. The business development team’s outreach depended on that data being right, and the manual process did not scale.

    The approach I explored a range of CRM, enrichment and AI tools, compared their costs and functionality, and picked the best fit for the situation. That meant building the collection step myself in Python and using Apollo, a sales intelligence platform, for enrichment.

    What I built For each company, the pipeline finds its leadership and board pages, pulls the text with open source browser automation tools and RAG frameworks, and has the Claude API extract names and titles. A role whitelist keeps only the positions the business development team tracks, and Apollo adds emails and LinkedIn profiles. ETL scripts then clean the results, remove duplicates, map locations to the CRM’s regions and reshape everything to its import format, so contacts merge in without creating duplicates.

    The result In the time left after the build, I ran the pipeline on a first batch of 211 firms. It found 2,042 contacts, an estimated 72% of them new to the CRM, and roughly 70% of those new contacts hold board seats, the group the CRM covered worst. The team uses it today, and the same pipeline can run the remaining firms. The firm projects it will save hundreds of working hours a year.

  2. May 2024 – Aug 2024

    Worcester, MA

    Research Associate

    Worcester Polytechnic Institute

    As a research associate in WPI’s NSF REU program, I tested six prompting strategies for getting ChatGPT to identify the stage of a pressure wound from a photo, to find out which is the most accurate and consistent.

    The problem Pressure injuries are staged by visual inspection, which is subjective, and wounds affect over 8 million people. The question was whether a general-purpose model like ChatGPT could stage them reliably, and how much the way it is prompted changes the answer.

    The approach I worked from the PIID dataset of 1,091 images across four severity stages and drew an 80-image sample, 20 per stage. I designed six prompts: Zero-Shot, Few-Shot, Chain of Thought, Tree of Thought, CO-STAR and TIDD-EC, and ran each in ChatGPT.

    What I built I wrote a Python script that scored every strategy on accuracy, precision, recall and F1 and produced confusion matrices. To test consistency, I chose two images per stage, one that clearly matched its stage and one an observer could easily mislabel, and ran every prompt on each image ten times.

    The result Chain of Thought was best on all four measures at 72.5% accuracy, and Few-Shot was the weakest. Early-stage wounds were the hardest to call: Chain of Thought scored 50% on Stage 1 but 90% on Stage 3. Small changes in wording shifted the answers, and adding medical context made them steadier. I presented the results at WPI’s NSF REU research symposium.

Unified Compensation Modeling Engine

Graduate Qualifying Project · Jan 2026 – May 2026

For my graduate capstone at WPI, sponsored by a medical device company, our four-person team replaced spreadsheet-based sales compensation modeling with a rule-driven system in Snowflake, and I built the Tableau dashboards that present its results.

The problem The sponsor’s compensation team designs sales incentive plans and has to test many versions of each against real sales data before rollout. They did this in spreadsheets with repeated formulas and inconsistent data, which was slow, error-prone and hard to scale.

The solution We built the system in Snowflake. Sales results sit in a star schema, and each plan’s rules live in a separate rules schema with header, detail and assignment layers. A calculation engine of stored procedures written in SQL scripting matches rules to sales, computes Tier 1, accelerator and bonus payouts, and unifies them into one output table, with validation checks along the way. My teammates built the Streamlit app where analysts enter plan parameters without writing SQL.

My part I designed and built the Tableau dashboards that read the engine’s output: a Payout Summary comparing current and proposed plans, a Most Important Metrics view of representative performance against plan over time, and a Monthly Cash Flow Summary showing when payouts land. The data model and calculation engine were a joint effort across all four of us.

The result The system covers the three standard payout types for four of the six franchises, the only four we had access to, since the other two were more complex and not modeled in Excel. The team expects it to save over 300 hours of work a year, and analysts can test a broader range of plan scenarios from consistent numbers before choosing one.

02

Edge-Based Smart Lock System

Jan 2025 – May 2025

In a WPI embedded machine learning course, our team built a touch-free door lock that listens for a wake word and reads a hand-signed code, and I compressed its models so they run fast on a Raspberry Pi.

The problem Keys and keypads do not work for everyone, including people with limited vision or mobility, and a lock has to run on a small, inexpensive computer with tight memory and speed limits.

The solution A Raspberry Pi with a microphone, camera and LCD listens for the wake word “House”, turns on the camera and reads a four-digit code signed by hand. Two CNNs do the recognizing: a wake word model trained on Google Speech Commands with MFCC features, and a sign-language model trained on a 27-class image set, using digits 0 to 9. Teammates trained the models and wrote the Pi software, and explored a binarized network and its hardware implementation with hls4ml, Yosys and OpenSTA.

My part I compressed the wake word model with TensorFlow Model Optimization and Lite-RT. I tested unstructured and structured pruning, dynamic range, float16, int16/int8 and full-integer quantization, weight clustering and quantization-aware training, and compared every version on size and accuracy.

The result Dynamic range quantization cut the model from 79 KB to 25 KB (68% smaller) and each prediction on the Pi from 108 to 36 microseconds, while accuracy rose from 96.1% to 98.3%. Pruning reached 10 KB at 94.5%. Full-integer quantization fell to 50% accuracy, and keeping int16 activations restored 98.3% at 28 KB.

03

Multimodal Stock Movement Prediction

Jan 2025 – May 2025

In a WPI deep learning course, our four-person team tested whether financial news improves stock-direction predictions beyond price history alone, and I built the price data work, the price-only models and the model that fuses news with prices.

The problem Most stock models look only at past prices. The question was whether news sentiment adds real predictive signal about whether a stock moves up or down, and whether combining the two beats either one alone.

The solution The team scored news headlines with FinBERT, paired them with S&P 500 price history from Yahoo Finance, and compared six models, from a naive baseline and a BiLSTM to FinBERT classifiers and transformers, before fusing price and sentiment in a dual-stream transformer. My teammates built and tuned the FinBERT sentiment models.

My part I started with the data: 15 S&P 500 stocks chosen to cover steady, volatile and flat behavior, with engineered features such as moving averages, RSI and MACD, checked with a random forest. I built the price-only models that set the baseline, a naive forecast, a BiLSTM and a multi-task transformer that predicts the next day, week and month from 30 days of features, and then the dual-stream transformer, with an autoencoder compressing the sentiment embeddings and focal loss for class imbalance.

The result The dual-stream model reached 92.7% accuracy for next-day direction and 97.81% for next-week, against 51.7% for the naive baseline on next-day. Sentiment data covered only about 25 days, so those numbers need a longer test window before they can be trusted.
