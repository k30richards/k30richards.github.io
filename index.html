02 Experience

  1. Jun 2026 – Aug 2026

    Boston, MA

    AI Intern

    Locust Walk

    I built an automated pipeline that finds the executives and board members at the companies the firm tracks and prepares them to load into its CRM, replacing contact research that was done by hand.

    The firm’s CRM data was incomplete and going stale, and keeping it current meant researching one contact at a time, which did not scale. For each company, the pipeline finds its leadership and board pages, pulls the page text (with Jina, or Playwright for pages that need a real browser), has the Claude API read it and extract every name and title, and keeps only the roles the business development team tracks by checking each title against a whitelist I wrote. Apollo then adds emails and LinkedIn profiles. The CRM could not take that output as it was, so I wrote ETL scripts that clean the results, remove duplicates, map each location to the CRM’s own region categories and reshape everything into its import format, so the contacts merge in without creating duplicate or conflicting records. Run across 211 firms, it found 2,042 contacts, an estimated 72% of them new to the CRM. The team uses it today, and the firm projects it will save hundreds of working hours a year. I also compared CRM and AI tools for the firm, including a cost model of its tool stack, and tested them on its real data. For example, I found that Clay fails silently on records missing a company domain and LinkedIn address, which helped the firm choose tools that would work at a low cost.

  2. May 2024 – Aug 2024

    Worcester, MA

    Research Associate

    Worcester Polytechnic Institute

    I tested six prompting strategies for getting ChatGPT to identify the stage of a pressure wound from a photo, to find out which one is the most accurate and consistent.

    Pressure injuries are staged by looking at them, which is subjective, so the question was whether an AI model could do it reliably and which way of asking works best. I worked from the PIID dataset of 1,091 wound images across four severity stages and took an 80-image sample, 20 from each stage. I designed six prompts: Zero-Shot (no examples), Few-Shot (a few examples), Chain of Thought (reason step by step), Tree of Thought (weigh several lines of reasoning) and two structured templates, CO-STAR and TIDD-EC. I ran each one in ChatGPT and wrote a Python script to score accuracy, precision, recall and F1 and to build confusion matrices. To test consistency, I picked two images from each stage, one that clearly matched its stage and one an observer could easily mislabel, and ran every prompt on each image ten times. Chain of Thought did best on all four measures, at 72.5% accuracy, and Few-Shot was the least reliable. Small changes in wording moved the answers, and adding medical context made them steadier, which is worth knowing before anyone relies on AI for medical images. I presented the results at WPI’s NSF REU research symposium.

01

Unified Compensation Modeling Engine

Graduate Qualifying Project · Jan 2026 – May 2026

Our four-person team replaced a medical device company’s spreadsheet-based sales compensation modeling with a rule-driven system in Snowflake, and I built the Tableau dashboards that turn its results into comparisons stakeholders can act on.

The sponsor’s compensation team designs sales incentive plans and has to test many versions of each plan against real sales data before one is chosen. They did that in spreadsheets full of repeated formulas and inconsistent data, which was slow and hard to trust. We replaced it with a system in Snowflake, designing and building the data model and calculation engine together as a team. Sales results sit in one central table (a star schema) tied to tables for territories, roles, products and time, so every comparison starts from the same numbers. Each plan’s rules are stored as data in three layers (the plan’s details, its payout rates and the reps it applies to), separate from the code, so a plan can change without rewriting anything. A calculation engine, built from stored procedures written with Snowflake scripting in SQL, works out who qualifies and what each rep is paid (base pay, accelerators and bonuses), with checks along the way. My teammates built the Streamlit app where analysts enter plan settings without writing SQL. I designed and built the Tableau dashboards on top of the results: one compares the current plan with proposed ones, one tracks representative performance against plan over time, and one shows when payouts land across the year. The system covers four of the six franchises and the three standard payout types, and the team expects it to save over 300 hours of work a year.

02

Edge-Based Smart Lock System

Jan 2025 – May 2025

Our team built a touch-free door lock that listens for a wake word and then reads a four-digit code signed by hand to a camera, and my part was shrinking the models so they run fast on a small Raspberry Pi.

Keys and keypads do not work for people with limited vision or mobility, so the team designed a lock that needs no touch. A Raspberry Pi 5 with a microphone, camera and small display listens for a wake word (“House”), then turns on the camera and reads the four-digit code signed with the hand, showing Locked or Unlocked on the display. Two neural networks do the recognizing: one for the wake word, trained on Google’s Speech Commands audio, and one for the hand signs, trained on a 27-class sign-language image set (we used digits 0 to 9). The rest of the team trained those models and wrote the Pi software, and also tested a binarized version of the wake word network (one bit per weight) and took it through chip-design tools to estimate how fast dedicated hardware could run it. My part was compression, because the models had to run quickly on very limited hardware. Using TensorFlow’s optimization tools and Lite-RT, I tried pruning (removing weights that matter little), several kinds of quantization (storing weights as small integers instead of 32-bit decimals), weight clustering (letting similar weights share a value) and quantization-aware training, and compared every version on size and accuracy. The best all-round result was 8-bit quantization: the wake word model dropped from 79 KB to 25 KB (68% smaller), accuracy rose from 96.1% to 98.3%, and each prediction on the Pi dropped from 108 to 36 microseconds. Pruning got it down to 10 KB at 94.5% accuracy, which gave the team a clear size-versus-accuracy menu to choose from.

03

Multimodal Stock Movement Prediction

Jan 2025 – May 2025

Our four-person team tested whether financial news improves stock direction predictions beyond what price history alone gives, and I built the price data work, the price-only models and the model that combines news with prices.

Most stock-prediction models only look at past prices, so the question was whether the news adds a real signal about whether a stock will go up or down. The team used S&P 500 prices from Yahoo Finance and news headlines scored by FinBERT, a language model trained on financial text, and my teammates built and tuned the FinBERT sentiment models. I started with the data: I picked 15 stocks that behave differently (five steady, five volatile and five flat, sorted by their volatility and total return) and engineered features such as moving averages, RSI and MACD, using a random forest to check which ones mattered. I then built the price-only models that set the bar: a naive baseline that predicts tomorrow will match today, a bidirectional LSTM, and a multi-task transformer that reads 30 days of features and predicts the next day, week and month. Finally I built the dual-stream transformer, which processes price and sentiment separately and lets them look at each other through cross-attention, with an autoencoder compressing the sentiment inputs and focal loss to handle the imbalance between up and down days. It scored 92.7% accuracy for next-day direction and 97.81% for next-week, well above the baselines, but the sentiment history covered only about 25 days, so those numbers need a longer test before anyone should trust them.
